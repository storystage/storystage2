# 7. The same scene, staged two ways

Learners stage one short scene from Shakespeare, or another play the class is studying, twice, with different choices of setting, camera and performance, and compare how the meaning changes.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Ages** | 11–13 (England KS3, Scotland S1–S2, Wales step 4, Northern Ireland Years 8–9) |
| **Template** | A scene from a play (`scene`): entrance, the clash, turning point, exit |
| **Time** | Four lessons of 50–60 minutes |
| **Groups** | Pairs; two pairs share a scene and stage it differently |
| **Prompter** | Explain only or Plan only. The Prompter never writes script lines; the play's words are the playwright's, and the staging is the learners' |
| **Curriculum** | `EN-EN-KS3-1`, `EN-EN-KS3-2`, `EN-EN-KS3-5`, `EN-CO-KS3-7`; `SC-EXA-x-14`, `SC-EXA-x-15`, `SC-ENG-x-31`; `WA-LLC-LI-4`, `WA-EA-CR-4`, `WA-EA-RE-4`; `NI-LL-KS3-2`, `NI-LL-KS3-3`, `NI-AR-KS3-DR`, `NI-LL-KS3-LO` |

## Learners will

- analyse a short scene: what each character wants, and where it turns;
- make deliberate staging choices (setting, lighting, camera distance, timing, movement) that support an interpretation;
- compare two stagings of the same words and explain how the choices change the meaning.

## Choosing the scene

Twenty to forty lines with two or three characters and a clear turning point: for example *Macbeth* Act 1 scene 7 (Macbeth and Lady Macbeth), *Romeo and Juliet* Act 3 scene 1 (the fight), or *A Midsummer Night's Dream* Act 3 scene 2 (the lovers' quarrel). Shakespeare's text is in the public domain; bring it in by typing it or with the Fountain import.

## Lesson 1: read the scene

1. Read the scene aloud in role. Mark the entrance, the clash, the turning point and the exit.
2. Choose the **A scene from a play** template and put the lines into the four parts.
3. Each pair in a shared scene picks a different interpretation (Lady Macbeth is in control; Lady Macbeth is desperate) and writes it as one sentence.

## Lesson 2: storyboard the interpretation

1. Frame each part with sketches: who is close to the camera, who is far away, who faces whom.
2. Choose the set and backdrop to fit the interpretation (a bright banqueting hall, or a dark corridor).

## Lesson 3: stage and direct

1. Set the stage and code the performance: entrances, where each character moves, the voices, pauses on the key lines, gestures.
2. Rehearse, check against the storyboard, and adjust the camera.

## Lesson 4: compare

1. Screen the two versions of each scene one after the other.
2. The class discusses: what does each version make us think about the characters? Which choices did that: the setting, the camera, the timing?
3. Each learner writes a short comparison, using evidence from both films.
4. Export with credits that name the playwright, and the models' sources.

## Support and stretch

- **Support:** a shorter extract (10–15 lines) and a modern-English summary alongside.
- **Stretch:** stage the same scene as a film and as a play seen from the audience's seats (one fixed wide camera), and compare. That is Northern Ireland's "how meanings change when texts are adapted to different media".

## What to look for

- Staging choices that are deliberate and fit a stated interpretation (England KS3 reading; `EXA 3-14a`).
- A comparison that ties each effect to a choice (Wales step 4 literature and responding; Northern Ireland KS3 media awareness).
- The words stay the playwright's; the meaning comes from the staging.
