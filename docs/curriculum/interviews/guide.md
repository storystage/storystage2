# Interview guide (WP16.7)

About 45 minutes. Timings are a guide; follow the teacher's story where it's useful, and use the prompts only if they're needed. **Bold** questions are the ones to keep if time runs short.

Before you start: consent form back; recording on only if they agreed; have Part C's pictures ready to share (below).

## Opening (3 minutes)

> Thank you for doing this. I'm working on StoryStage, a free tool for making 3D animated films with code, for ages 6 to 13. I'm not here to sell it. I want to understand your teaching: what you're trying to do, and what gets in the way. There are no right answers, and nothing you say will be linked to your name or school. Is it still all right to record? You can stop at any time.

## Part A: their context (5 minutes)

1. **What do you teach, to which ages, and in which nation's curriculum?**
2. What does a typical week of that teaching look like: how many lessons, how long, how many pupils?
3. What devices do pupils use in those lessons, and how often can they get them?

*Prompts:* shared or one each? Laptops, Chromebooks, iPads? (Don't go deep: the IT questions are elsewhere.)

## Part B: the job, in their words (17 minutes)

This is the heart of the interview. Get one real, recent example and stay with it.

4. **Think of the last time you taught pupils to make something that told a story, or to code something. What was it?**
   - *Prompts:* which year group? What did they make? When was it?
5. **What made you decide to do that project, then?**
   - *Prompts:* a scheme of work, an inspection, a pupil's idea, a gap you'd noticed, a deadline (a show, an assembly, a parents' evening)?
6. **Walk me through it, from planning to the end. What did you use?**
   - *Prompts:* which tools, apps or materials? Paper storyboards? Scratch? Something else? How many lessons did it take?
7. **What was hardest about it, for you and for the pupils?**
   - *Prompts:* time, devices, confidence, behaviour, pupils who finish early or fall behind, getting the work back next lesson, marking it.
8. **What did you do about that?** (The workarounds they invented show what really matters.)
9. **How did you know it had gone well? What did the best work look like?**
   - *Prompts:* what did you show the head, the parents, an inspector? What did you mark, and how?
10. **If you did it again next term, what would you want to be different?**
11. **Is there anything you've stopped using, or would never use again? Why?** (What they "fired" shows what they won't accept.)
12. Who else has a say in whether you can use something new: a computing lead, the head, IT, parents?

**End of Part B: read back the job in their words.** For example: "So what you need is something that lets your Year 4s plan a story and code it in about four lessons, on the Chromebooks you get on Thursdays, and gives you something to show at the open evening. Is that right?" Note how they correct you.

## Part C: show StoryStage (12 minutes)

Share these pictures one at a time (from `docs/design/direction-a/screenshots/`):

1. `01-storyboard.png`: the Storyboard.
2. `02-production-stage-blocks.png`: the Production stage, with the code and the Prompter's plan.
3. `03-screening-room.png`: the Screening room, with the check against the storyboard.

Say only: "StoryStage works in three steps, like a film studio: plan the film on a storyboard, build and code each scene, then watch it, check it against the plan and share it." Then ask:

13. **Where, if anywhere, would this fit in what you described earlier?** (Let them say "it wouldn't". That's useful.)
14. **What would worry you about using it with your class?**
15. A scene's code stays closed until the scene has been storyboarded. **Would that help your pupils, or get in their way?**
16. Show the four story templates (tale, myth, scene from a play, explain how it works; `docs/curriculum/arc-templates.md`). Which would you use? Is there a shape of story or explanation you'd want that isn't there?
17. Show the one use case closest to their subject (`docs/curriculum/use-cases/`). Would it work in your lessons as written? What would you change: timing, groups, the steps?
18. Ages: StoryStage is designed for 6 to 13. **Which of your classes could use it? Who would find it too hard, or too young for them?**

## Part D: the Prompter, and assessment (6 minutes)

19. StoryStage has an optional AI helper, the Prompter. It explains, asks questions about the pupil's story, and suggests a plan before it builds anything. It never writes the story, and it's off unless an adult sets it up. You choose one of four levels for the class: off, explain only, plan only (pupils build every block), or full. **Which would you choose, for which class, and why?**
20. Would you, your school or a parent pay for the AI's use, or would you only use it without? (In StoryStage 1, a request cost about 10 to 30 US cents, roughly 8p to 25p; explanations will use a cheaper model. Give the figure only if asked.)
21. **What would you need from StoryStage to assess the work?** (For example: the storyboard next to the film; the code; the check's results; a record of what the AI did.)

## Close (2 minutes)

22. Is there anything I should have asked and didn't?
23. Would you be willing to look at a few lesson plans, or try StoryStage with a class next spring? (The pilot, 19.3; no commitment now.)

> Thank you. I'll write this up without your name or school. If you'd like to see the write-up, or withdraw what you've said, just let me know.

## Notes for the interviewer

- **Listen for their words.** Note the words they use for things ("planning", "storyboard", "algorithm", "debugging", "the show"); they feed the wording on screen and in the teacher guide.
- **Don't defend the design.** If they dislike storyboard first or the Prompter, ask what they'd want instead, and why.
- **Count, don't guess.** "About four lessons" is better than "a few"; "eight Chromebooks for 30 pupils" is better than "not many".
- **Leave out pupils' names,** and anything that would identify the school.
